Wind damage

Wind is the peril that actually drives roof claims around Washington. It is also the one where the industry still argues about what counts. Here is the mechanism, the evidence, and the argument.

Wind does not pull nails out

Almost every asphalt shingle made in the last fifty years is self-sealing. A strip of heat-activated adhesive runs across the shingle, and once the sun warms the roof the strip bonds the bottom edge of each shingle to the course underneath. That bond, not the nails, is what holds the roof surface down in a storm.

The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety has tested this directly and concluded that the strength of the seal between shingles is the single most important factor in how asphalt shingles perform in high wind. Wind damage, in other words, is usually a bond failure.

Progressive unsealing

The current working theory is that wind rarely tears a fully bonded shingle off in one go. It breaks the seal a little at a time, across repeated loading, until enough of the strip has let go that the shingle can flutter. Once it flutters it can fold back on itself, crease, and tear.

IBHS field surveys after storms in Florida and Texas found shingles that had lost adhesion along part of their sealant strips, and similar patterns of insufficient adhesion turned up on 22 of the 27 roofs they looked at. IBHS is careful to call this a working theory with research still running, and its 2025 research agenda commits to further work on whether the existing wind test standards need changing to account for it. We are careful about it too.

Heat is part of the story. Work published through ASCE found that heat exposure can reduce uplift resistance along the sealant strip in some products, though many shingles carry enough excess bond capacity to stay above the design requirement. The authors landed on progressive unsealing rather than loss of raw strength as the culprit.

Where the shingle actually breaks

Forensic work from Haag Engineering puts the failure point at the top of the exposed section, just below the butt end of the shingle lying over it. That line is the hinge. When a tab lifts and folds back, that is where it creases, and the crease is often hidden underneath the overlapping shingle, where a surface look will not find it.

What an adjuster will accept

Four signs are accepted by effectively everyone on both sides of a claim:

  • Creased or folded shingles. A permanent fold line across the tab.
  • Torn shingles. Any tear through the mat.
  • Missing shingles or missing portions of shingles.
  • Exposed or backed-out fasteners where a tab has lifted off them.

Those are the easy cases. The hard case is a shingle whose seal has let go but which shows none of those marks, and that case is genuinely contested.

The argument worth knowing about

A 2017 paper by Haag authors, published through IIBEC, took the position that a sealed shingle affected by wind leaves lasting evidence in the form of creases, folds, tears, or missing material. On that reading, a shingle that is merely unadhered, with no such mark, is not evidence of wind damage. This is the position most carrier-retained engineers apply.

Policyholder-side analysts dispute it. Their argument is that the crease forms at that hidden pivot underneath the overlapping shingle, so a surface inspection can easily miss it. The Haag paper itself acknowledges the crease can be hard to see on newer, pliable shingles, and suggests putting a hand underneath to find it.

We take no view on who is right. What matters on your roof is practical: if a shingle has unsealed, the underside has to be checked by hand before anyone concludes there is no crease. That is the step that gets skipped, and it is the step that decides the claim.

What gets called wind damage and is not

Carriers deny these routinely, and in most cases they are right to:

  • Installation-caused unsealing. Research cited by Haag, originating at the University of Florida, points to three installation faults that stop the strip bonding: nails driven into the sealant strip itself, debris caught in the strip, and the release tape left stuck to it. Unsealing in a distinct, repeating pattern tends to mean the roof was never sealed properly.
  • Age and heat cycling. Seals degrade over years of thermal movement. That is wear and tear, and wear and tear is excluded from a homeowners policy as a matter of course.
  • Shingles that never sealed at all, because they were laid in cold weather and the strip never reached activation temperature.

The ratings printed on the bundle

Two ASTM standards show up on shingle packaging, and they measure different things.

The numbers have moved between editions of the standard, which is why a figure quoted from an old datasheet may not match a current one. If a rating matters to you, ask which edition it came from.

The scope limit nobody mentions

D7158 classes assume Exposure Category B or C, a mean roof height no greater than 60 feet, and no topographic speed-up. Outside those conditions the class does not simply apply, and a project-specific calculation is needed. A house on an exposed ridge line is not the building the standard had in mind.

ASTM D3161 is the older tunnel test, used for shingles without seal strips. Class A is 60 mph, Class D is 90 mph, Class F is 110 mph. It carries no adjustment for building height or exposure, so its numbers do not translate to in-service wind speeds. Treat it as a way to compare products. It is not a promise about your roof.

Warranty speed is not damage threshold

Manufacturers publish wind warranty tiers, and homeowners read them as predictions. They are not. They are contract terms, and every one of them is conditional. The higher tier generally requires a specific nail count per shingle and the manufacturer's own starter strip at eaves and rakes, and some premium tiers require several matched accessories from the same brand. Miss one condition and the higher tier does not apply, whatever the shingle was rated for on the shelf.

Local code may also require more nails than the manufacturer's minimum. Worth asking your contractor which standard they built to, and getting the answer in writing.

What the wind actually does here

Around Washington, wind is not one peril among several. It is the peril.

The National Weather Service office at Blacksburg keeps the most complete severe weather climatology published for Virginia, covering 1950 to 2013 and 6,601 severe reports. Its coverage area is southwest and western Virginia, so read it for shape and treat the counts as indicative. In that record, convective wind accounts for 76.5% of all severe reports, hail for 21.1%, and tornadoes for 2.3%.

  • Severe convective wind runs May through August, which covers 80% of reports, peaking from late June into July.
  • 70% of events land between 2pm and 8pm, with the sharpest concentration from 2pm to 6pm.

The derecho, and what it measured

On 29 June 2012 a derecho crossed the region and became the benchmark everyone still refers to. The measured gusts at our airports were 71 mph at Dulles, 70 mph at Reagan National, and 66 mph at BWI. The storm had produced 91 mph at Fort Wayne and 82 mph at Dayton earlier in its run.

That distinction matters, because the event is often described as an 80-plus mph storm. It was, across its whole footprint. Here it measured in the high 60s and low 70s. Thirteen people were killed by the winds, mostly by falling trees, and roughly four million customers lost power, some for a week. The last comparable derecho to reach the DC and Baltimore area before that was in June 2008.

Why roof-level wind beats the number on the forecast

During Hurricane Isabel in 2003, wind sensors mounted on building tops around DC recorded 70 to 85 mph while surface winds ran at roughly half that, with stronger gusts mixing down from above in bursts. Your roof sits in the faster air. A reported ground-level gust is not the load your shingles saw.

What to do after a wind event

  • Stay off the roof. Walking an asphalt roof in warm weather dents the asphalt, which inspectors call marring, and scuffs granules along your route. Neither is functional damage, and an adjuster who finds them has grounds to read the roof as damaged by something other than the storm. You can manufacture your own denial in ten minutes.
  • Photograph from the ground, and from a window or a neighbour's upper floor if you have one. Get the whole slope in frame as well as close shots.
  • Report it promptly. Policies require notice as soon as reasonably possible, and long delays give carriers a late-notice argument.
  • Do not make permanent repairs before the insurer has inspected. Temporary measures to stop water coming in are a different matter and are normally reimbursable. Keep the receipts.
  • Get the inspection in writing, with photographs, and make sure whoever does it has lifted and checked the underside of any unsealed shingles.

Where to read your own policy

Two sections decide what you are owed. The loss settlement method section tells you whether you hold replacement cost or actual cash value cover. The declarations page tells you whether a separate wind, hail, or named-storm deductible applies, and whether it is a flat sum or a percentage of the dwelling limit.

The Maryland Insurance Administration publishes the clearest worked example of why the second one matters. On $3,000 of wind damage to a home insured for $100,000: a $1,000 flat wind deductible leaves the insurer paying $2,000; a 2% hurricane deductible leaves them paying $1,000; a 5% hurricane deductible leaves them paying nothing at all, because the deductible exceeds the repair cost.

A Virginia deadline that is easy to miss

Virginia's settlement regulation lets you take an actual cash value payment first and claim the difference later. That later claim has to be filed within six months of the last actual cash value payment or of a final court order. It is a short window and it is separate from the policy's own suit clause, which is typically a good deal longer. Read both.

If a contractor offers to cover your deductible

Decline it, and take it as a signal about everything else they have told you. Virginia's consumer protection law bars a contractor from knowingly paying, waiving or rebating an insurance deductible on owner-occupied residential property, and from offering anything of value in exchange for a roof inspection or for filing a roof claim. Maryland treats covering a deductible with intent to defraud the insurer as a fraudulent insurance act. The Maryland Insurance Administration goes further and names "free roof" offers outright as part of a fraud scheme in which wear and tear gets presented to a carrier as storm damage.

Virginia also requires a good-faith itemised estimate before you sign any repair authorisation, and a warning in bold on the signature page telling you to verify coverage with your insurer first. If a contract arrives without them, that is worth noticing.
