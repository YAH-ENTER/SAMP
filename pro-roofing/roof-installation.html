Service 04  New builds and additions

New roof
installation

New construction, additions, dormers and garages. The details that decide whether a roof lasts get worked out on paper before anything is nailed down.

What gets decided before the first bundle

A new roof is the one chance to get the assembly right while everything is still accessible. Three things matter more than the shingle you pick.

Ventilation

An attic needs intake at the soffit and exhaust at the ridge, roughly balanced. Get it wrong and the roof runs hot, which cooks the shingles from underneath, drives the blistering that later gets mistaken for hail damage, and in winter melts snow unevenly so water backs up at the eaves. Exhaust without intake is worse than neither, because it pulls conditioned air out of the house.

Flashing, planned rather than improvised

Every place the roof changes direction or meets something else is a detail that has to be designed: valleys, sidewalls, headwalls, chimneys, skylights, and the junction where a lower roof dies into a wall. These are where roofs leak, and on new work there is no excuse for sorting them out on the day.

Wind rating, and what it is conditional on

The rating printed on the bundle assumes a particular nail count, the manufacturer’s own starter strip at eaves and rakes, and in the upper tiers a set of matched accessories. It also assumes standard conditions: the ASTM D7158 classes are written for Exposure Category B or C with a mean roof height no greater than sixty feet and no topographic speed-up. A house on an exposed ridge line is outside those assumptions and needs the calculation done, not the class taken at face value.

Coordinating with the builder

On new construction we would rather be involved while the framing is up and the sheathing is going on. It is the only point at which deck fastening, ventilation paths and flashing substrates can still be changed cheaply. Bringing a roofer in the week before the shingles arrive means working round decisions already made.

What we install

  • Architectural asphalt shingle, which is what most houses here get and what the market is built around.
  • Impact-rated asphalt shingle, worth discussing if hail is a concern. Be aware that impact rating and premium are not the same thing, and that an impact class is a laboratory result and not a prediction about a claim. Our hail article covers what the testing does and does not show.
  • Standing seam metal, where the budget and the hold period justify it. Longest service life available, and the easiest surface to add solar to later without penetrating the weather plane.
  • Low-slope assemblies on porch roofs, dormer tie-ins and flat sections, which need a membrane rather than shingle. Anything under the shingle manufacturer’s minimum pitch is not a shingle roof, whatever it looks like from the street.

For commercial work and the full range of low-slope systems, see the commercial roofing guide.

Solar, later

If there is any chance of panels going on, say so now. Mounting to standing seam clamps to the seam and penetrates nothing. Mounting to shingle means penetrations, and every one has to be flashed as carefully as a plumbing vent. Planning for it at install is cheap. Retrofitting the planning is not.
