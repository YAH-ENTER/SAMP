Tree falls

Around Washington this is how most serious roof damage actually happens. Not uplift, not hail. A mature tree in a mature canopy, on a lot where somebody once dug a trench near the roots.

If a tree is on your house right now

Get everyone out if the tree has broken into living space, if any wall looks leaning or shifted, or if you can hear cracking or settling. Treat every line near the tree as live and call 911 and the utility. If you smell gas, leave without touching a switch.

Then read the section on removal before anyone starts cutting. The tree may be the only thing holding your roof up.

Why trees come down

Virginia Cooperative Extension sorts storm damage to trees into six kinds: blow-over, crown twist, stem failure, root failure, branch failure, and lightning. Most of what lands on houses here is the first and the fourth.

Trees are built for wind. They sway under load and recover in the calm, and over several growing seasons that movement builds what foresters call wind firmness, with the wood thickening in response to the loads it keeps meeting. A tree that has never been allowed to move, because it was staked too tightly or for too long after planting, never develops it.

What turns a healthy tree into a falling one

  • Root damage from construction. This is the big one in suburban Northern Virginia and Maryland, and Virginia Tech is blunt about it. Construction damage to roots is very common, it leaves fewer roots carrying the same tree, and a tree in that condition can be pulled over by "little more than mild weather events." Trenching for utilities, regrading, a new driveway, an addition. The damage happens years before the failure.
  • Old injuries. Trees do not heal, they seal. An injured tree walls the wound off and grows around it, so the old and new wood at that point stays structurally weaker than the rest. Under wind load, that is the weak link.
  • Lopsided crowns. Very few crowns are symmetrical and some are markedly one-sided. That loads one side harder and literally twists the main branches and trunk, and the twisting concentrates at any existing weak point.
  • Branch attachment. A branch is held to the trunk only by successive layers of thin wood at the collar. It makes branches flexible and, from the tree's point of view, disposable. Branches become much more vulnerable once ice has added weight.
  • Species and included bark. Faster-growing trees have softer, weaker wood and break more readily. Some species carry bark grown into the branch union, which makes the union weak from the start. Decay sitting at an included-bark union is a particularly poor combination.

Why oaks and lightning go badly together

Smooth-barked trees such as beech and birch wet in a continuous sheet, so a strike tends to run down the outside of the bark in the water. Rough-barked trees, which is most oaks, wet only in patches with dry crevices in between, so the bolt travels inside the tree through the sap channels. The heat there reaches around 50,000 °F and the sap flashes to steam, blowing the channels apart above and below ground.

Tulip poplar

Worth naming specifically, because the region is full of them. Tulip poplar is among the tallest hardwoods in the east and it is listed among species of special concern, with many planted trees blowing down in storms and a reputation for weak wood. Holding that much mass that high means a failure arrives with a long lever arm behind it.

Documented local failures follow the pattern you would expect. A large tulip poplar at Maymont in Richmond came down after heavy rain, with waterlogged ground as the likely trigger, and was found to be almost completely hollow. A multi-trunk poplar in Reston lost one trunk in a thunderstorm and took the tops off three neighbouring trees on the way down.

The regional picture

Pull the primary records together and the story is consistent: in this region the dominant severe peril is wind, and the dominant path from wind to roof damage is trees.

  • The 29 June 2012 derecho killed thirteen people with its winds, and the National Weather Service assessment records that they died mainly from falling trees. Around four million customers lost power, some for a week.
  • During Hurricane Isabel in September 2003, over 700 trees came down in the District alone, and 23 of them landed on homes. Only two inches of rain fell. The damage was wind and trees, not water from above.
  • Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 brought trees down across the region, closed streets in DC, and killed a driver in Maryland when a large tree fell on a moving car.
  • The remnants of Ida in September 2021 put a tree into a Maryland home among widespread tree and power pole damage.

Set that against a mature canopy holding weak-wooded poplars and old oaks, on lots where root systems have been cut into for decades of building work, and the risk is structural rather than unlucky.

What a strike actually does

What you see from the driveway is a hole. What matters is underneath it, and it is normally worse than the hole suggests.

  • The decking fails first, and the break usually runs wider than the visible opening because the sheathing splits along its fastening lines.
  • Rafters or truss members snap or are driven out of plane. A truss is an engineered assembly; once one member breaks, the load it was carrying goes somewhere that was never designed for it.
  • The ridge deflects. A dip in the ridge line after a strike is a structural signal, not a cosmetic one.
  • Walls take load they were not sized for. Roof loads travel down a specific path. Break the path and the remaining route may be a wall, a header or a beam that was never in that calculation.
  • Water gets in immediately, and keeps getting in through insulation and ceilings for as long as the opening is unsealed.

All of which is why the inspection after a tree strike is an attic and structure inspection as much as a roof one.

When a house stops being safe, and the removal trap

There is no FEMA rule that tells a homeowner their house is uninhabitable. What exists is the professional framework building officials use. ATC-45, the Applied Technology Council's field manual for safety evaluation of buildings after windstorms and floods, produces one of three placards: INSPECTED (green), RESTRICTED USE (yellow), or UNSAFE (red). It was developed with IBHS, its audience is inspectors and engineers, and the placards and evaluation forms can be downloaded free.

In practice, a local official or a structural engineer makes that call. Real cases follow it: a Massachusetts fire chief declared a house structurally unsound after a tree came through the roof, and a Colorado family of five was displaced when a strike left their home uninhabitable.

The counterintuitive part, and the one that hurts people

A tree lying across a damaged roof is often partially supporting the framing it broke. Lift it off without shoring first, and the moment of collapse is the moment of removal, not the moment of impact.

This is why "we will have it off by this afternoon" from a crew with a crane and no structural assessment is the wrong answer. The sequence is assess, shore, then remove.

Before anyone goes back in

  • Assume every line touching or near the tree is energised. A line that is not sparking is not a line that is dead.
  • If gas is detected, get out without operating any switch, and call the utility from outside.
  • If water is coming through onto outlets, fixtures or a panel, kill the relevant circuits at the panel before entering that area.
  • If you are not certain the house is safe, stay somewhere else and have it assessed. Your policy may reimburse temporary accommodation while the home is uninhabitable.

The insurance part

A tree falling on a house in a storm is normally a covered loss, minus the deductible. Cause of loss is what matters. A claim can be denied if the homeowner brought the tree down themselves through reckless action, such as trying to fell it without a professional.

The debris removal clause

This is the one that catches people, and it is worth knowing before the adjuster arrives. Many policies cap debris removal at a sublimit, commonly $500, $1,000 or $2,000 per occurrence. In a documented dispute, an adjuster applied that cap to the cost of getting the tree off the roof.

The argument against that reading, made in the trade press, is twofold: the debris removal cap is written for removal from the residence premises rather than from the damaged structure, and taking a tree off a roof is a reasonable repair to protect the property from further damage, which is a separate coverage grant from debris removal. That is an argument to raise, not a settled rule, but it is a specific and useful one if you find the cap being applied to the crane bill.

What to do, in order

  • Photograph everything before anything moves, including the whole tree. Once it is cut up and carted off, the evidence of what fell and how it landed is gone.
  • Notify the insurer.
  • Do emergency mitigation and keep every receipt. Tarping and boarding up protect the house and contents from rain, animals and theft, and these costs are almost always reimbursable.
  • Do not make permanent repairs until the claim amount is agreed. The DC regulator says the same: wait for the insurer's inspection, keep receipts for temporary work.
  • If the offer comes in low, appeal it and submit contractor bids showing the real cost. You can request an independent appraisal and ask for a claim review.

Two deadlines to put in your calendar

Report promptly. Policies require notice as soon as reasonably possible, and courts have treated repairs made before notice as a specific source of prejudice to the insurer.

In Virginia, if you take an actual cash value payment first and intend to claim the difference later, that difference has to be claimed within six months of the last such payment. That window is separate from, and much shorter than, your policy's suit clause.

What we do on a tree call

We are roofers, not an arborist and not a structural engineer, and on a serious strike you will need all three. What we do is the roof and the assembly under it.

  • Emergency dry-in, so water stops entering while everything else gets sorted out.
  • A written assessment of the decking, rafters or trusses, and the load path, with photographs, and a direct recommendation if an engineer needs to be involved.
  • A scope and estimate itemised the way a carrier expects to read it.
  • The permanent repair, once the claim is agreed and any structural work is signed off.

We will also tell you if the damage is smaller than it looks. That happens more often than people expect, particularly where a limb came down and not a trunk.
