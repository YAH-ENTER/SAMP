Hail damage

Most of what homeowners point at after a storm is not hail damage. Some of what they walk past is. The difference is physical, it is checkable, and it has a lot more to do with the back of the shingle than the front.

A bruise is a fracture you can feel

Hail does not usually punch a hole in an asphalt shingle. It fractures the fibreglass mat inside it, leaving a dent that feels soft under your thumb. Inspectors describe it as the bruise on an apple, and that is close to exact. Granules at the strike point are knocked loose, so the asphalt underneath shows through as a darker spot.

The confirming evidence is not on the top of the shingle. It is underneath. A genuine hail impact usually leaves an observable fracture on the back face of the shingle, and that is the check that settles the question. Once a soft spot is found on the surface, the shingle gets lifted and the underside is read for fracture lines.

A field rule worth knowing

The hailstone was roughly twice the diameter of the mark it left. A half-inch impact mark points to a stone around an inch across. It is approximate, but it is a sanity check against what the weather record says fell on your street that day.

Why it matters that bruises rarely leak

A fresh hail bruise does not normally produce a leak. It shortens the life of the shingle. Forensic engineers are explicit about this, and it explains something that frustrates homeowners: a carrier will not accept "my roof is not leaking" as evidence that nothing is wrong, and will not accept "there are marks on my roof" as evidence that something is.

What both sides work from is functional damage: a puncture or fracture that compromises the roof's ability to shed water or that measurably shortens its service life. Everything in a hail claim turns on that phrase.

How big the stones have to be

Sources genuinely disagree here, so here is the range. A single confident number would be invented.

Published thresholds for functional damage to asphalt shingles
ThresholdAttributed to
Under 1 inch rarely causes functional damageMarshall 1999, via a 2025 review in Frontiers in Materials

The honest reading: one inch is roughly the floor below which functional damage is unlikely, and the engineering literature puts reliable failure of asphalt shingles somewhere in the 1.5 to 2 inch range. Age, product, and how well the deck supports the shingle move that a long way in either direction.

For metal roofs, gutters, vents and other soft metals, there is no authoritative size threshold we could find. Sources describe denting qualitatively and treat dented soft metal as corroboration that the storm was severe enough to matter, rather than as a damage test in itself. Anyone quoting you a precise number for metal is quoting something they made up.

The test square

When an adjuster climbs your roof, the assessment method traces back to a protocol written by Timothy Marshall and Richard Herzog of Haag Engineering and published through the NRCA. What follows is how the method is described in published inspector training; the protocol document itself is not freely available, so treat this as a reliable account of practice, not a quotation of the standard itself.

  • A test square is ten feet by ten feet, one hundred square feet. Where the roof geometry will not allow a square, another shape is used, but the area stays at a hundred square feet.
  • The size and placement do not change with the roof covering.
  • One square per slope, placed in the area with the most damage.
  • Areas shaded by overhanging branches are excluded, because the canopy changed what reached the roof.
  • Only hits meeting the functional damage definition get counted.

The eight-hit rule is not a rule

Eight hits per square is widely described as a common threshold for totalling a slope. It is a carrier convention, not an industry standard. Different insurers use different numbers, some use an undisclosed formula, and roof age feeds into whether the decision lands on one slope or the whole roof. If someone tells you eight is the magic number, ask which carrier they mean.

There is fair criticism of the method worth knowing. A test square is a sample, not a measurement. Where the adjuster places it changes the answer, and a subjective placement can both miss damage that exists elsewhere and overstate a locally bad patch. Carriers also differ in how they translate counts into a decision.

Spatter marks, and why waiting costs you

This is the most useful thing in the article, and almost nobody tells homeowners about it.

When hail strikes an oxidised or algae-darkened surface, it cleans a mark into it. These spatter marks have a rounded head and a fading tail. The head's diameter approximates the size of the stone that made it. The tail points the direction the stone was travelling. Together they tell an inspector how big the hail was and which way the storm came through, which is how this storm's damage gets separated from an older storm that arrived from a different angle.

The evidence evaporates

Spatter marks are explicitly a temporary condition. They fade. They are not damage in themselves, they are the dating evidence, and they go away on their own over the following weeks and months. If you are going to have a roof looked at after a hailstorm, the argument for doing it soon is not urgency for its own sake. It is that the part of the record which proves when is the part that disappears first.

Storms are directional. One elevation, occasionally two adjacent ones, take the near head-on impacts and the energy that goes with them. Surfaces angled away take glancing blows or nothing at all. An inspection that only looks at one slope has not finished.

Different materials record a strike differently: circular indentations in metal, circular or half-moon fractures in vinyl, chips or scuffs in wood, vertical fractures or punctures in wood shakes, and fracturing or shattering in concrete tile.

What impact ratings actually measure

UL 2218 is a steel ball drop test. Four classes, four ball sizes: Class 1 at 1.25 inches, Class 2 at 1.5, Class 3 at 1.75, Class 4 at 2 inches, dropped from twelve to twenty feet to match the kinetic energy a hailstone that size would carry in a thunderstorm.

The pass and fail criterion is the part people get wrong. A product fails if there is any evidence of opening on the back of the shingle, meaning tearing, fracturing, cracking or rupturing. If the back face is intact, it passes. Crushed granules, dents, and openings visible only on the top surface are not failures under UL 2218. The test measures rupture, not appearance.

The IBHS result that should temper expectations

In 2014 IBHS bought 22 asphalt shingle products from five manufacturers through ordinary local vendors, built them into 3-foot panels to each manufacturer's own instructions, struck each product twice at multiple locations, and examined the marks under a microscope at 7.5x.

None of the products tested, impact-rated or not, passed more than Class 2 impacts without at least one double-impact location failing the UL 2218 criteria. Class 4 products are supposed to withstand all four ball sizes without the back face cracking.

  • Impact-rated products did clearly outperform basic ones. Impact-rated architectural shingles passed around 75% of Class 1 locations against roughly 55% for basic architectural.
  • Premium is not the same as impact-rated. Premium architectural products beat basic ones at every class, and still came in below the impact-rated products. Thickness and weight do not substitute for an impact rating.
  • Impact resistance comes either from a mesh or scrim bonded to the back of the shingle, or from polymer modifiers in the asphalt. The second type looks identical to an ordinary shingle from above.

Two caveats IBHS states itself

First, the performance criteria in UL 2218 do not necessarily reflect the criteria an insurer uses when deciding to repair or replace a roof after hail. A UL class does not predict a claim outcome.

Second, and this one is genuinely useful when you are inspecting: the most severe denting occurred where impacts landed directly over a brace rather than between framing. The stiffened deck could not flex, so the shingle absorbed everything. On a real roof that means damage concentrates over the rafters and trusses. It is a good place to look first.

The four things mistaken for hail

Blistering

Heat drives gases out of the asphalt, and the surrounding layers trap them into blisters. When a blister pops it leaves a pockmark that reads, at a glance, exactly like a hail strike. Two things separate them. A blister has no bruise, so it does not feel soft the way an impact does. And in a blister the mat is visible inside the void, whereas a hail strike exposes the mat with granules still scattered around it. Blistering is treated by manufacturers as a cosmetic manufacturing condition, and poor attic ventilation makes it worse.

Foot traffic

Two signatures, and both get denied. Scuffing is granule loss along a path somebody walked. Marring is a dent pressed into the asphalt while it was warm and soft, and marring is explicitly not considered functional damage. New, steep and hot roofs mark most easily.

The discriminator is pattern. Hail lands randomly across a slope. Foot traffic goes somewhere on purpose: from the access point to the chimney, around a vent, along the ridge. A line of marks with a destination is not weather.

Mechanical and tool damage

Damage caused during installation looks very like hail on a casual inspection. The tell is uniformity, because hail is directional and random while installation damage tends to be consistent across the whole roof. Deliberate damage has its own signature: scraping with a coin edge leaves grooves and removes material, where hail indents it.

Ordinary granule loss

Shingles shed granules from the day they are made, through shipping, installation and every year afterwards. Weathering shows up as broad, irregular thinning across a slope. Hail shows up as round, localised spots. There is a live disagreement about granule loss on its own: manufacturers have published bulletins saying granules lost to hail mean shingle life lost, while other sources treat granule loss with no exposed asphalt as weak evidence and want to see bruising and mat fracture before calling it damage. Expect that argument if your claim rests on granule loss alone.

How much hail we actually get

We would rather tell you this than have you find it out from an adjuster.

One forensic engineering firm lists Washington DC, alongside New York and Boston, among low-severity hail regions, and names pre-existing damage in such regions as a condition commonly mistaken for hail. That is not a reason to skip an inspection. It is a reason to be exact about evidence, because a carrier working here starts from a lower prior than one working in Texas.

Real severe hail does fall here. From the Virginia climatology published by the National Weather Service at Blacksburg, which covers the southwest of the state. Read it for shape; the counts are indicative:

  • Severe hail, meaning one inch or larger, runs April through July, which covers 83% of reports, peaking in late May and early June.
  • Under 1% of severe hail reports fall between October and February.
  • 78% of severe hail reports happen between 2pm and 8pm.
  • 45% of all hail reports in that record are an inch or larger.

Locally documented stones give a sense of scale: 1.75 inch hail at Upperville and near Criglersville in August 2021, and 1.75 inch near Bel Air, Maryland in June 2010.

Worth being straight about: in this region wind is the dominant severe peril and hail is secondary. In the Virginia record above, convective wind made up 76.5% of severe reports against 21.1% for hail. If a contractor knocks on your door after a storm insisting you have hail damage, the base rate is not on their side.

What to do, and what not to

  • Do not climb up. You will scuff granules and mar warm asphalt, and both read as non-storm damage to an adjuster.
  • Photograph the soft metal. Gutters, downspouts and vent caps record impacts more readily than shingles do and corroborate the size of what fell.
  • Mark suspected damage with chalk, never with permanent marker or spray paint. Chalk washes off. Paint is now a defect on your roof.
  • Get it looked at while the spatter marks are still there.
  • Do not make permanent repairs before the insurer inspects. Emergency measures to stop water are fine and are normally reimbursable, so keep receipts.
  • Read your declarations page for a separate wind and hail deductible, and your policy's loss settlement section for whether you hold replacement cost or actual cash value.

Replacement cost against actual cash value

The DC regulator's worked example is the clearest one published. A ten-year-old roof costing $25,000 to replace, depreciated 50%: under replacement cost cover you receive $25,000 less the deductible. Under actual cash value you receive $12,500, and you find the rest.

Even under replacement cost, most policies pay in two parts. You get the depreciated amount first and the withheld depreciation once the work is finished. If you never do the work, you never see the second cheque.
